# How AI is Transforming What Lawyers Actually Do The fundamental nature of legal work is changing. Here's what that means
for your career.

### How AI is Transforming What Lawyers Actually Do
#### *The fundamental nature of legal work is changing. Here's what that means for your career.*
In 2020, a second-year associate at a major law firm spent three days
reviewing 400 contracts to identify termination provisions. She billed
24 hours. The client paid \$12,000.

In 2025, the same associate completes the same task in four hours. She
identifies all termination provisions, flags unusual terms, and has time
to draft a memo analyzing the implications. The client gets better work
product. The associate has time to take on additional projects.

What changed? AI.

But this isn't a story about automation replacing lawyers. It's a story
about what happens when you remove the tedious parts of legal work and
free lawyers to do what they do best: reason, analyze, and advise.


The shift from research to reasoning is the most significant
transformation in legal practice since Westlaw digitized case law in the
1970s. If you're a lawyer --- or training to become
one --- understanding this shift isn't optional. It's essential.

### What Legal Work Used to Look Like
Ask a senior partner to describe what associates did in the 1990s:

"You'd send them to the library for days to find cases on specific
issues. They'd come back with stacks of photocopies. You'd tell them to
summarize the key holdings. They'd spend more days reading and writing
summaries. Then you'd review their work, correct misunderstandings, and
finally have something useful."

Or ask about due diligence:

"We'd assign teams of associates to review thousands of documents.
They'd spend weeks in data rooms, taking notes on material agreements,
flagging issues, creating summaries. It was mind-numbing but necessary."

Or discovery:

"First-year associates would review documents for months. Privileged,
not privileged. Responsive, not responsive. It was expensive for clients
and awful for associates, but somebody had to do it."

The pattern is consistent: much of legal work consisted of finding,
organizing, and summarizing information. This work required legal
training --- you needed to know what was relevant --- but it didn't
require advanced legal analysis.

It was research. Not reasoning.

### What AI Changed
AI didn't eliminate the need for research. It dramatically accelerated
it.

Contract review: AI can read 400 contracts and extract all termination
provisions in minutes. It can compare them to your standard language and
flag deviations. What took days now takes hours.

Legal research: AI can analyze hundreds of cases and identify those most
relevant to your issue in seconds. It can organize them by jurisdiction,
date, and holding. What took days now takes hours.

Discovery: AI can categorize thousands of documents by topic, flag
potentially privileged communications, and identify key documents for
detailed review. What took months now takes weeks.

Due diligence: AI can extract key terms from hundreds of agreements,
identify unusual provisions, and flag potential risks. What took weeks
now takes days.

Notice the pattern: AI handles the finding and organizing. What takes
longer is the next step --- analyzing what you found and determining
what it means for your client.

This is the shift: from spending most of your time finding information
to spending most of your time interpreting it.

From research to reasoning.

### What This Means for Junior Lawyers
The immediate concern: "If AI does the research work that junior
associates used to do, what happens to junior associates?"

It's a legitimate question. But the answer isn't "they're eliminated."
It's "their work changes."

#### Old Model: Research-Focused
Year 1--2: Document review, due diligence, cite-checking, basic
research.\
Year 3--4: More complex research, first drafts of memos and briefs.\
Year 5--7: Client contact, managing junior associates, case strategy.\
Year 8+: Partner track --- business development, client relationships.

The pyramid depended on large numbers of junior associates doing
high-volume research and document review to generate billable hours.

#### New Model: Reasoning-Focused
Year 1--2: AI-assisted research and document review, with focus on
analysis and issue-spotting. Instead of spending weeks reviewing
documents manually, you spend days using AI to flag issues and analyzing
what the flagged issues mean.

Year 3--4: Client-facing work earlier. Since you're not buried in
research, you have time to attend client meetings, draft strategy memos,
and participate in case planning.

Year 5--7: Sophisticated analysis, negotiation, and strategy. You're
doing the work that used to be reserved for senior associates and junior
partners.

Year 8+: The work hasn't changed much --- but you're better prepared
because you've been doing higher-level work since year 1.

The trajectory is steeper. You develop reasoning and judgment skills
faster because you're not spending years doing primarily research and
document review.

[Wei Shen](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=74rTDM3rkj4) of USC Gould School of Law conducted research comparing
AI to human lawyers in contract review. His findings: AI outperformed
humans on certain review tasks. But the solution isn't fewer
lawyers --- it's lawyers focused on what humans do better than AI.

### What This Means for Established Lawyers
If you've been practicing for 10+ years, you've already made the
transition from research-focused to reasoning-focused work. AI
accelerates work you might have delegated to junior associates --- but
it doesn't fundamentally change your role.

Or does it?

The competitive pressure is real. Professor Daniel Schwarcz's research
at the University of Minnesota Law School found that lawyers using AI
with retrieval-augmented generation produced significantly
higher-quality work in less time --- not just faster work, but better
work.

That means:

For transactional lawyers: The lawyer who can review 50 contracts in the
time it takes you to review 10 wins the work.

For litigators: The lawyer who can analyze 10,000 discovery documents in
a week while you're still working through 2,000 has a competitive
advantage.

For regulatory lawyers: The lawyer who can monitor regulatory
developments across multiple jurisdictions and alert clients immediately
provides more value than the lawyer who produces quarterly summaries.

This isn't hypothetical. It's happening now.

Thomson Reuters reports that 77% of legal professionals use AI for
document review. Those who don't are competing against those who
do --- and losing.

### What Reasoning Actually Means
If AI handles research, what's left for lawyers?

Everything that requires judgment:

#### 1. Creative Legal Strategy
AI is trained on past cases. It suggests arguments that have already
been made. Novel legal theories --- applying existing law to new
situations, analogizing to precedents from unrelated areas, crafting
test cases to develop new law --- require human imagination.

Professor Daniel Marcus of NYU Law: "The talks of AI being able to
replace creative jobs --- I don't think that'll ever happen. I don't
think AI will ever replace the human imagination."

#### 2. Client Counseling
Understanding a client's business, risk tolerance, and strategic
objectives requires relationship and judgment. So does explaining
complex legal issues in terms the client understands, helping them weigh
options, and providing reassurance when needed.

Max Junestrand, CEO of Legora, frames this as empowerment: his company's
co-founder's roommate needed legal help but couldn't afford a lawyer. AI
can democratize access to basic legal information --- but complex
matters still require human lawyers who understand the client's full
situation.

#### 3. Negotiation
Reading the room. Understanding counterparty incentives. Knowing when to
push and when to compromise. Building relationships. These are human
skills AI can't replicate.

#### 4. Ethical Judgment
Recognizing conflicts of interest. Determining when privilege applies.
Deciding whether to disclose adverse authority. Balancing zealous
advocacy against candor to the tribunal. These require professional
judgment that AI lacks.

#### 5. Advocacy
Crafting persuasive narratives. Connecting with juries. Adapting
arguments in response to judges' questions. These require human
communication skills.

[Mark
Cohen](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IME8_KB7wrg), CEO of Legal Mosaic and 50-year legal industry
veteran, summarizes: "There are machines that know probably more and can
spit out more in 10 minutes than I learned in 50 years of being a
slightly above average lawyer... But understand that we are a persuasion
business."

Persuasion is human.

### The Widening Gap
Here's what's actually happening in legal practice:

Group A lawyers have mastered AI-assisted research. They use it
routinely for document review, legal research, drafting, and due
diligence. They work faster and produce more consistent results.

Group B lawyers haven't adopted AI. They're still doing research and
document review the old way.

The productivity gap between Group A and Group B isn't 10% or 20%.
Professor Schwarcz's research found productivity gains of 38% to 140%
depending on the task.

That means a lawyer using AI effectively can produce twice as much
work --- or more --- in the same time.

What happens to Group B?

They lose work. They fall behind on promotions. They struggle to compete
for clients who expect AI-enabled efficiency. They find themselves
working longer hours to keep up with peers producing more in less time.

[Jaromir
Savelka](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZX2RU_OU-5Y) of Carnegie Mellon's School of Computer Science
observes: "We're at an inflection point. The trajectory is clear." AI
adoption in legal practice will continue accelerating.

The divide between lawyers who adapt and lawyers who resist will widen.

### What Legal Education Must Change
Law schools are beginning to recognize this shift --- slowly.

Traditional legal education teaches how to research --- finding cases,
analyzing statutes, understanding legislative history --- how to analyze
by applying law to facts, distinguishing cases, and constructing
arguments, and how to write memos, briefs, and contracts.

In a world where AI handles research and first drafts, legal education
needs to emphasize how to use AI effectively through prompting,
verification, and understanding capabilities and limitations. Students
need training in verifying AI output by distinguishing reliable from
unreliable information and checking sources. Advanced analytical skills
become paramount: strategic thinking, creative legal theories, and
making judgment calls that AI cannot. Client interaction
skills --- communication, counseling, relationship-building --- must be
developed early. Professional responsibility in the AI era requires
understanding confidentiality with AI tools, verification duties, and
disclosure obligations.

Some law schools are adapting. Most aren't --- yet.

If you're currently in law school, don't wait for your curriculum to
change. Learn these skills independently. The gap between graduates who
understand AI and graduates who don't will be stark.

### What Firms Must Change
Law firm business models were built on leverage: junior associates doing
research and document review generated billable hours that partners
could mark up.

When AI compresses research time by 50--75%, the pyramid breaks.

Firms face a choice:

Option A: Pass savings to clients. Charge for 10 hours of AI-assisted
work instead of 40 hours of manual work. Compete on value and
efficiency.

Option B: Maintain billing. Charge the same for AI-accelerated work,
pocketing the efficiency gain as increased profit.

Option C: Redeploy time. Use time saved on research for higher-value
work --- strategic counseling, client development, complex
analysis --- that commands higher rates.

Most firms are trying Option B (secretly) while claiming Option A
(publicly). This is unsustainable.

Clients are sophisticated. When they learn that document review that
cost \$50,000 last year costs \$10,000 this year because of AI, they'll
demand the lower rate.

Forward-thinking firms are choosing Option C: redeploying time saved to
higher-value work.

Allen & Overy, a global law firm, piloted Harvey AI across multiple
practice groups. Their focus wasn't reducing headcount --- it was
enabling lawyers to provide better client service by eliminating
time-consuming research.

[Rupp
Pfalzgraf](https://www.lexisnexis.com/en-us/products/lexis-plus-ai.page), a boutique firm, implemented Lexis+ AI
and found that AI-assisted research didn't reduce the need for
lawyers --- it enabled them to provide more thorough analysis in less
time.

These firms recognize that AI is a competitive advantage, not a
cost-cutting measure.

### The Path Forward
If you're building a legal career in the AI era, here's what you need to
do:

#### 1. Master AI-Assisted Workflows Now
Don't wait for your firm to implement AI officially. Start experimenting
with low-risk applications: use AI to summarize articles or cases you're
already reading (verifying accuracy), draft routine emails or memos with
AI assistance (reviewing and revising), and organize research results
with AI (verifying citations independently). Build muscle memory for
AI-assisted work through consistent practice on tasks where mistakes are
easily caught.

#### 2. Develop Judgment Skills Actively
The work that can't be automated is judgment. Seek client contact early
in your career. Volunteer for strategic planning discussions. Practice
explaining complex legal issues simply. Learn to identify creative
solutions to novel problems. These skills will differentiate you in an
AI-augmented profession.

#### 3. Understand the Technology
You don't need to be a computer scientist, but you need to understand
how AI works through statistical prediction rather than understanding,
what AI does well in pattern matching, summarization, and organization,
what AI does poorly regarding judgment, creativity, and verification,
and when to trust AI output for verifiable tasks versus when not to for
judgment calls. This understanding will guide every decision about using
AI.

#### 4. Build Your Professional Brand Around Reasoning
As research becomes commoditized, reasoning becomes valuable. Position
yourself as someone who solves complex problems, provides strategic
advice, sees connections others miss, and counsels clients effectively.
These are the skills that command premium rates.

### The Bottom Line
Legal practice is shifting from research to reasoning. AI handles
finding and organizing information. Lawyers handle interpreting and
applying it.

This shift creates winners and losers:

Winners: Lawyers who embrace AI-assisted workflows, develop strong
reasoning and judgment skills, and focus on work that requires human
creativity and relationship.

Losers: Lawyers who resist AI, compete on research capabilities that are
being automated, and fail to develop judgment skills that can't be
automated.

The transformation isn't coming --- it's here. 77% of legal
professionals already use AI for document review. Adoption will only
accelerate.

The question isn't whether legal work will change. It's whether you'll
change with it.

From research to reasoning. From hours spent finding information to
hours spent interpreting it. From competing on who can review the most
documents to competing on who provides the best analysis and advice.

The shift is underway.
